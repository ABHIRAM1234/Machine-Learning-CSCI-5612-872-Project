Fire in Focus: Analyzing the Causes and Patterns of Wildfires

The Nature of the Topic

Does climate change, human activity, and environmental factors influence the frequency and severity of wildfires, and how can predictive models help mitigate these impacts on health, infrastructure, and biodiversity?

The rise in global temperatures and the increasing frequency of extreme weather events have led to an alarming increase in wildfires worldwide. In 2020, wildfires burned more than 10 million acres in the United States alone, affecting thousands of homes, displacing entire communities, and causing millions of dollars in damages. The causes of these fires are complex and multifaceted, driven by both natural factors and human-induced climate change. High temperatures, prolonged droughts, and mismanagement of natural resources have contributed to the spread and intensity of these fires, particularly in regions like California, where wildfires have become a recurring annual event. The impacts of wildfires extend beyond the immediate destruction of land and property; they also severely affect air quality, with smoke and particulate matter (PM 2.5) causing respiratory issues, heart disease, and exacerbating conditions like asthma. In addition, the environmental destruction caused by wildfires disrupts ecosystems, leading to biodiversity loss and long-term soil degradation. In 2020, over 1 million acres of agricultural land were lost to wildfires in California, highlighting the economic consequences as well. This project seeks to explore how data analysis and predictive models, such as machine learning and satellite imagery, can help forecast wildfire outbreaks, enabling better preparation and response to mitigate the devastating effects on human health, infrastructure, and ecosystems

The worst wildfire in the United States, which broke out in California in 2020, highlighted the urgent need for effective wildfire prediction.

Why Wildfire Analysis is Important

Wildfires are a growing concern, exacerbated by climate change and the increasing vulnerability of highly urbanized areas. The risk of wildfires is high in the United States, especially across grasslands, forests, and agricultural land, which can lead to devastating consequences including desertification, soil erosion, and loss of agricultural productivity. Moreover, wildfires alter landscapes, damage infrastructure, and harm biodiversity, making it essential to develop effective fire prevention and mitigation strategies.

Beyond the environmental impact, wildfires have far-reaching economic and social consequences. This project aims to help communities prepare for wildfires by predicting when and where they are likely to occur, thus safeguarding human life, natural habitats, and economic activities. The long-term goal is to protect both people and wildlife, reducing the impact of these devastating events.

Who is Affected?

Wildfires affect not just the immediate area but also entire regions, displacing people, destroying homes, and impacting mental health. Communities living near wildlands are particularly vulnerable. For firefighters and emergency responders, the physical and psychological toll is immense, often leading to exhaustion, burnout, and PTSD. Firefighters risk their lives daily to protect people and the environment, working under extreme conditions with limited resources. It's crucial to provide support for these heroes, both during and after their service.

Governments play a crucial role in managing the aftermath of a wildfire. They are responsible for making decisions related to evacuations, resource deployment, and providing public safety. Moreover, long-term recovery efforts can take years and require significant investments in rebuilding infrastructure. This project highlights the importance of interagency cooperation in managing these efforts efficiently.

Wildfires also devastate ecosystems, displacing wildlife and reducing biodiversity. Studies from events such as the 2020 Australia bushfires, where nearly 3 billion animals were killed or displaced, show the far-reaching consequences for animal populations and ecosystems. Soil erosion, water runoff, and reduced air quality further compound these impacts, emphasizing the need for comprehensive ecosystem management strategies that consider both human and environmental factors.

Industries such as agriculture, tourism, and real estate are also significantly impacted by wildfires. Farmers face the loss of crops and livestock, leading to economic hardships, while tourism can suffer as natural attractions become damaged or inaccessible. Real estate markets may also decline, especially in areas at high risk for future fires. Understanding these interconnected impacts is key to developing effective risk management strategies.

What has been done so far, and what are the gaps?

While significant progress has been made in wildfire prediction, much work remains to address the growing threat. Satellite imagery, remote sensing, and machine learning algorithms are transforming the way wildfire data is collected and analyzed. However, there are gaps in integrating real-time data from multiple sources, and many existing models do not consider human factors, such as evacuation patterns. Furthermore, predicting fire behavior is still a challenging task due to the complexities of the environment and climate variability.

Data Collection

Data was collected from two APIs, NASA FIRMS API and Open-Meteo.

The Fire Information for Resource Management System (FIRMS) provides near real-time satellite data on active fires and thermal anomalies globally. Utilizing observations from NASA's MODIS and VIIRS instruments, FIRMS delivers data within hours of satellite overpasses, enabling timely monitoring of wildfire activity. This information is crucial for resource managers, researchers, and the public to assess fire locations, extents, and intensities. FIRMS offers various data formats, including CSV, KML, and shapefiles, and supports web services like WMS and WFS for seamless integration into mapping applications.

Open-Meteo is an open-source weather API that provides free access to weather forecasts and historical data without requiring an API key. It offers high-resolution forecasts, ranging from 1 to 11 kilometers, by partnering with national weather services. The API delivers current weather conditions, hourly forecasts for up to 48 hours, daily forecasts for up to 8 days, and historical weather data spanning over 80 years. Open-Meteo's user-friendly JSON API facilitates easy integration into applications, making it a valuable resource for developers and researchers seeking accurate and accessible weather information.

By leveraging these APIs, the project effectively combines real-time fire data with detailed weather information, enhancing the analysis and understanding of wildfire patterns and their environmental impacts.

MODIS plays a vital role in monitoring land cover, sea surface temperatures, atmospheric properties, and more

VIIRS on SNPP continues the legacy of MODIS, offering enhanced capabilities for monitoring Earth's surface, atmosphere, and oceans

It carries an advanced VIIRS instrument with similar spectral bands and spatial resolutions as its predecessors. NOAA-21's VIIRS enhances the continuity and quality of environmental data records, supporting improved weather forecasting and climate monitoring

    Principal Component Analysis (PCA)

    Principal Component Analysis (PCA) is a powerful statistical technique used for dimensionality reduction while preserving the most significant variance in a dataset. It transforms high-dimensional data into a smaller set of principal components—orthogonal directions capturing the highest variance. PCA enhances data visualization, reduces complexity, and improves computational efficiency by leveraging eigenvalue decomposition of the covariance matrix or Singular Value Decomposition (SVD).

    PCA in This Project

    PCA was applied to a wildfire dataset to analyze high-dimensional features and reduce complexity while retaining critical variance. The goal was to visualize patterns and determine how many dimensions are necessary to capture meaningful information.     Clustering is an unsupervised learning technique used to group similar data points together. This section compares three popular clustering algorithms: K-Means, Hierarchical Clustering, and DBSCAN.

    Understanding Distance Metrics

    Distance metrics play a crucial role in clustering by determining the similarity between data points. The choice of metric significantly impacts the clustering results:

    • Euclidean Distance: The most common metric, used in K-Means, measuring straight-line distance between two points.
    • Manhattan Distance: Measures distance along axes at right angles, useful for high-dimensional spaces.
    • Cosine Similarity: Used in Hierarchical clustering, evaluates the angle between vectors rather than distance.
    • Density-Based Distance: Applied in DBSCAN, where clusters are identified based on density variations.

    Comparison of Clustering Algorithms

    Clustering Type Description Strengths Weaknesses
    K-Means Partitions data into k clusters by minimizing intra-cluster variance. Works well for spherical clusters, fast, scalable. Needs predefined k, sensitive to outliers, struggles with non-spherical clusters.
    Hierarchical Creates a tree of clusters using agglomerative or divisive methods. No need to predefine k, produces dendrograms for visual analysis. Computationally expensive for large datasets, best for smaller datasets.
    DBSCAN Groups points based on density and identifies outliers as noise. Finds arbitrarily shaped clusters, robust to noise and outliers. Struggles with varying densities, sensitive to eps and min_samples selection.

    Association Rule Mining (ARM)

    Association Rule Mining (ARM) is a data mining technique used to discover interesting relationships between items in large datasets. It is widely used in market basket analysis, recommendation systems, and various fields where relationships between data points are valuable.

    Key Metrics in ARM

    • Support: The frequency of an itemset appearing in the dataset.
    • Confidence: The likelihood that the consequent occurs given the antecedent.
    • Lift: The strength of association, measuring how much more likely items co-occur compared to random chance.

    Apriori Algorithm and FP-Growth

    The Apriori algorithm generates frequent itemsets and prunes them based on minimum support. However, it can be computationally expensive for large datasets. To improve efficiency, the FP-Growth algorithm is used in this project because it compresses transactions into an FP-tree, making it significantly faster than Apriori for large datasets.

    How ARM Was Used in This Project

    In this project, Association Rule Mining was used to identify environmental and weather conditions that frequently occur together and may signal increased wildfire risk. For example, rules such as "High wind and low soil moisture → fire occurrence" help us uncover potentially hazardous patterns that might not be immediately obvious. By analyzing these occurrences, the model helps provide a better understanding and anticipating fire-prone scenarios.

    Data Preparation

    ARM requires only unlabeled transactional data. In this project, the dataset was formatted into a binary transaction format, where each row represents a set of items purchased together or occurring together in an event.

    Overview

    Naïve Bayes (NB) is a family of simple yet powerful classification algorithms based on Bayes' Theorem, with the fundamental assumption that features are conditionally independent given the class label. Despite this "naïve" assumption, Naïve Bayes models often perform surprisingly well in real-world applications, especially in high-dimensional spaces such as text classification.

    Common use cases for Naïve Bayes include:

    • Text classification (e.g., spam detection, sentiment analysis)
    • Medical diagnosis
    • Document categorization
    • Recommender systems

    Advantages of Naïve Bayes models include fast training, scalability to large datasets, ease of interpretation, and relatively good performance even with limited data.

    Types of Naïve Bayes Classifiers in Scikit-learn

    Scikit-learn provides multiple variants of Naïve Bayes classifiers, each tailored for different types of input features:

    • Gaussian Naïve Bayes (GNB): Assumes that features follow a normal (Gaussian) distribution. Best suited for continuous, normally-distributed data. Common applications include sensor-based analytics and biometric classification.
    • Multinomial Naïve Bayes (MNB): Designed for discrete count features such as word frequencies. Ideal for text classification, including spam detection, news categorization, and topic modeling.
    • Bernoulli Naïve Bayes: Suitable for binary features (e.g., presence/absence). Works well with binary text data after binarization. Common in sentiment analysis and email filtering.
    • Categorical Naïve Bayes (CNB): Handles features that take on a limited number of discrete values. Useful for encoded categorical data such as demographics or survey responses.

    Comparison Summary

    Variant Best for Feature Type Example Use Case
    Gaussian NB Continuous data Normally distributed features Medical diagnosis, sensor data
    Multinomial NB Text classification Count-based discrete features Email spam detection
    Bernoulli NB Binary data Boolean features Sentiment classification (yes/no features)
    Categorical NB Categorical variables Labeled categories Demographic-based classification

    Smoothing in Naïve Bayes

    Smoothing (typically Laplace smoothing) is essential in Naïve Bayes to handle the issue of zero probabilities. If a feature value never appears in the training data for a given class, it will result in a probability of zero, which can entirely nullify the prediction. Smoothing ensures that even unseen values have a small non-zero probability, improving generalization.

    Overview

    Linear Regression is a machine learning algorithm used to predict a continuous value based on input features. It fits a straight line through the data by finding the best values for the slope and intercept that minimize prediction errors. The line represents the relationship between inputs and the output. The model is trained using a method called least squares, which reduces the difference between actual and predicted values

    Logistic Regression: Logistic regression is used to predict categories, typically yes/no or 0/1 outcomes. It calculates a linear combination of the input features and passes the result through a sigmoid function to get a probability. This probability is used to classify the input. The closer the output is to 1, the more confident the model is in predicting the positive class.

    Both use a linear combination of input features to make predictions. The difference is in what they predict: linear regression outputs numbers, logistic regression outputs probabilities for categories. Linear regression uses least squares to fit a line, while logistic regression uses a method called maximum likelihood to find the best model.

    Logistic regression uses the sigmoid function to convert the linear result into a probability between 0 and 1. This makes it possible to classify inputs based on how likely they belong to a certain category.

    Logistic regression is trained using maximum likelihood estimation, which means it finds the model parameters that make the observed data most probable. This method adjusts the model so that the predicted probabilities are as close as possible to the actual class labels in the training data.

    Overview

    A Decision Tree is a supervised learning algorithm used for both classification and regression tasks. It works by splitting data into branches based on feature values, forming a tree-like structure where each internal node represents a decision based on an attribute, each branch a possible outcome, and each leaf a final prediction (class label or value).

    A simple decision tree used to decide whether to play badminton based on the weather

    Decision Trees are easy to understand and visualize, making them useful for interpreting data patterns and explaining model decisions. They can handle both numerical and categorical data and require minimal data preprocessing.

    Image 2: Visual of how a dataset is split based on features.

    To decide the best feature to split on at each node, Decision Trees use metrics like GINI Index, Entropy, and Information Gain:

    • Entropy measures impurity or randomness in the dataset. A pure set has entropy 0.
    • Information Gain measures the reduction in entropy after a split. Higher gain means a better split.
    • GINI Index is another impurity measure, often used as an alternative to entropy in classification tasks.

    Example:

    Suppose we have a small dataset to decide if a student studies based on whether it’s sunny:

    Total examples: 10 (6 "Yes", 4 "No")
    Entropy before split = - (6/10) log₂(6/10) - (4/10) log₂(4/10) ≈ 0.971

    After splitting on weather:
    - Sunny: 4 "Yes", 1 "No" → Entropy ≈ 0.722
    - Not Sunny: 2 "Yes", 3 "No" → Entropy ≈ 0.971

    Weighted average entropy after split ≈ 0.846
    Information Gain = 0.971 - 0.846 = 0.125
    This tells us that splitting on weather gives us some improvement, but maybe not the best.

    It is generally possible to create an infinite number of trees because each feature can be split in many ways, especially with numerical data. Without restrictions like maximum depth or minimum samples per leaf, the tree can keep growing, potentially overfitting the training data. That's why pruning or setting limits is important to keep the model generalizable.
