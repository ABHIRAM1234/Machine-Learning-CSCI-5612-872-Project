Fire in Focus: Analyzing the Causes and Patterns of Wildfires

The Nature of the Topic

Does climate change, human activity, and environmental factors influence the frequency and severity of wildfires, and how can predictive models help mitigate these impacts on health, infrastructure, and biodiversity?

The rise in global temperatures and the increasing frequency of extreme weather events have led to an alarming increase in wildfires worldwide. In 2020, wildfires burned more than 10 million acres in the United States alone, affecting thousands of homes, displacing entire communities, and causing millions of dollars in damages. The causes of these fires are complex and multifaceted, driven by both natural factors and human-induced climate change. High temperatures, prolonged droughts, and mismanagement of natural resources have contributed to the spread and intensity of these fires, particularly in regions like California, where wildfires have become a recurring annual event. The impacts of wildfires extend beyond the immediate destruction of land and property; they also severely affect air quality, with smoke and particulate matter (PM 2.5) causing respiratory issues, heart disease, and exacerbating conditions like asthma. In addition, the environmental destruction caused by wildfires disrupts ecosystems, leading to biodiversity loss and long-term soil degradation. In 2020, over 1 million acres of agricultural land were lost to wildfires in California, highlighting the economic consequences as well. This project seeks to explore how data analysis and predictive models, such as machine learning and satellite imagery, can help forecast wildfire outbreaks, enabling better preparation and response to mitigate the devastating effects on human health, infrastructure, and ecosystems

Why Wildfire Analysis is Important

Wildfires are a growing concern, exacerbated by climate change and the increasing vulnerability of highly urbanized areas. The risk of wildfires is high in the United States, especially across grasslands, forests, and agricultural land, which can lead to devastating consequences including desertification, soil erosion, and loss of agricultural productivity. Moreover, wildfires alter landscapes, damage infrastructure, and harm biodiversity, making it essential to develop effective fire prevention and mitigation strategies.

Beyond the environmental impact, wildfires have far-reaching economic and social consequences. This project aims to help communities prepare for wildfires by predicting when and where they are likely to occur, thus safeguarding human life, natural habitats, and economic activities. The long-term goal is to protect both people and wildlife, reducing the impact of these devastating events.

Data Collection

Data was collected from two APIs, NASA FIRMS API and Open-Meteo.

The Fire Information for Resource Management System (FIRMS) provides near real-time satellite data on active fires and thermal anomalies globally. Utilizing observations from NASA's MODIS and VIIRS instruments, FIRMS delivers data within hours of satellite overpasses, enabling timely monitoring of wildfire activity. This information is crucial for resource managers, researchers, and the public to assess fire locations, extents, and intensities. FIRMS offers various data formats, including CSV, KML, and shapefiles, and supports web services like WMS and WFS for seamless integration into mapping applications.

Open-Meteo is an open-source weather API that provides free access to weather forecasts and historical data without requiring an API key. It offers high-resolution forecasts, ranging from 1 to 11 kilometers, by partnering with national weather services. The API delivers current weather conditions, hourly forecasts for up to 48 hours, daily forecasts for up to 8 days, and historical weather data spanning over 80 years. Open-Meteo's user-friendly JSON API facilitates easy integration into applications, making it a valuable resource for developers and researchers seeking accurate and accessible weather information.

By leveraging these APIs, the project effectively combines real-time fire data with detailed weather information, enhancing the analysis and understanding of wildfire patterns and their environmental impacts.

    MODIS: Launched in 1999, MODIS is a scanning radiometer that captures data in 36 spectral bands, ranging from visible to thermal infrared wavelengths. It offers global coverage with a spatial resolution of 250 to 1000 meters, depending on the spectral band. MODIS plays a vital role in monitoring land cover, sea surface temperatures, atmospheric properties, and more

    VIIRS on Suomi NPP (SNPP): Launched in 2011, the Suomi National Polar-orbiting Partnership (SNPP) satellite carries the VIIRS instrument, which features 22 spectral bands spanning from 412 nm to 12 µm. It provides global coverage with a swath width of 3060 km and a spatial resolution of 375 to 750 meters, depending on the spectral band. VIIRS on SNPP continues the legacy of MODIS, offering enhanced capabilities for monitoring Earth's surface, atmosphere, and oceans

    VIIRS on NOAA-21: Launched in November 2022, NOAA-21 is the latest addition to the Joint Polar Satellite System (JPSS) series. It carries an advanced VIIRS instrument with similar spectral bands and spatial resolutions as its predecessors. NOAA-21's VIIRS enhances the continuity and quality of environmental data records, supporting improved weather forecasting and climate monitoring

    Principal Component Analysis (PCA)

    Principal Component Analysis (PCA) is a powerful statistical technique used for dimensionality reduction while preserving the most significant variance in a dataset. It transforms high-dimensional data into a smaller set of principal components—orthogonal directions capturing the highest variance. PCA enhances data visualization, reduces complexity, and improves computational efficiency by leveraging eigenvalue decomposition of the covariance matrix or Singular Value Decomposition (SVD).

    PCA in This Project

    PCA was applied to a wildfire dataset to analyze high-dimensional features and reduce complexity while retaining critical variance.     Clustering is an unsupervised learning technique used to group similar data points together. This section compares three popular clustering algorithms: K-Means, Hierarchical Clustering, and DBSCAN.

    Understanding Distance Metrics

    Distance metrics play a crucial role in clustering by determining the similarity between data points. The choice of metric significantly impacts the clustering results:

    • Euclidean Distance: The most common metric, used in K-Means, measuring straight-line distance between two points.
    • Manhattan Distance: Measures distance along axes at right angles, useful for high-dimensional spaces.
    • Cosine Similarity: Used in Hierarchical clustering, evaluates the angle between vectors rather than distance.
    • Density-Based Distance: Applied in DBSCAN, where clusters are identified based on density variations.

    Comparison of Clustering Algorithms

    Clustering Type Description Strengths Weaknesses
    K-Means Partitions data into k clusters by minimizing intra-cluster variance. Works well for spherical clusters, fast, scalable. Needs predefined k, sensitive to outliers, struggles with non-spherical clusters.
    Hierarchical Creates a tree of clusters using agglomerative or divisive methods. No need to predefine k, produces dendrograms for visual analysis. Computationally expensive for large datasets, best for smaller datasets.
    DBSCAN Groups points based on density and identifies outliers as noise. Finds arbitrarily shaped clusters, robust to noise and outliers. Struggles with varying densities, sensitive to eps and min_samples selection.

    Association Rule Mining (ARM)

    Association Rule Mining (ARM) is a data mining method used to uncover relationships or patterns among variables in large datasets. It is commonly applied in market basket analysis, recommender systems, and environmental monitoring.

    What is ARM and How Does It Work?

    ARM finds frequent patterns in transactional data and generates rules in the form of {antecedent} → {consequent}. Each rule is evaluated based on support, confidence, and lift, which measure frequency and strength of association.

    ARM Workflow: frequent itemsets → rules using Support, Confidence, and Lift

    Working of Apriori Algorithm

    Key Metrics in ARM

    • Support: The proportion of transactions that contain an itemset.
    • Confidence: The probability that the consequent is found given the antecedent.
    • Lift: The ratio of observed confidence to expected confidence. Lift > 1 indicates a positive association.

    Apriori vs FP-Growth

    While Apriori systematically explores all item combinations, it can be slow for large datasets. FP-Growth is more efficient and uses a compressed FP-tree structure to mine frequent itemsets without candidate generation.

    How ARM Was Used in This Project

    In this wildfire prediction project, ARM was used to discover recurring weather and environmental conditions that frequently co-occur before fires. For example, rules such as {"low humidity", "high wind"} → {"fire"} help us pinpoint high-risk patterns that are not immediately visible using supervised learning methods.

    Overview

    Naïve Bayes (NB) is a family of probabilistic classifiers based on Bayes’ Theorem with the assumption that features are conditionally independent given the class. Despite this simplification, Naïve Bayes models perform well in many real-world applications, particularly when working with high-dimensional data.

    Naïve Bayes is commonly used for:

    • Text classification (spam detection, sentiment analysis)
    • Medical diagnosis
    • Document categorization
    • Recommender systems

    Advantages: Fast to train, simple to implement, works well on small datasets, and scalable to large feature spaces.

    Types of Naïve Bayes Classifiers in Scikit-learn

    Scikit-learn provides multiple Naïve Bayes variants, each suited to specific feature types:

    • Multinomial Naïve Bayes (MNB): Designed for count data such as word frequencies in text documents. Suitable for NLP tasks like spam detection or topic classification.
    • Gaussian Naïve Bayes (GNB): Assumes features are normally distributed. Works best with continuous features like temperature or sensor readings.
    • Bernoulli Naïve Bayes: Best for binary/boolean features (e.g., presence or absence of a word). Used in binary text data and sentiment analysis with binary indicators.
    • Categorical Naïve Bayes (CNB): Works with discrete categorical features (e.g., color: red, blue). Ideal for demographic or ordinal-encoded data.

    Visual representation of Naïve Bayes structure and classification flow

    Comparison Summary

    Variant Best for Feature Type Example Use Case
    Gaussian NB Continuous data Normally distributed features Medical diagnosis, sensor data
    Multinomial NB Text classification Count-based discrete features Email spam detection
    Bernoulli NB Binary data Boolean features Sentiment classification (yes/no features)
    Categorical NB Categorical variables Labeled categories Demographic-based classification

    Smoothing in Naïve Bayes

    Why smoothing? Smoothing addresses the problem of zero probability. If a feature value is not seen in the training data for a given class, the model assigns a zero probability to it — making the entire product of probabilities zero and the classification fail.

    Laplace Smoothing (additive smoothing) adds a small constant (usually 1) to all counts to ensure no probability is exactly zero. This improves generalization and model robustness on unseen data.

    Laplace Smoothing for Naive Bayes Classifier

    Data Preparation

    I implemented and evaluated three types of Naïve Bayes classifiers on a wildfire dataset with binary labels (fire = 0 or 1): MultinomialNB, GaussianNB, and CategoricalNB. Since each model requires specific data formats, I applied different preprocessing techniques to ensure compatibility:

    • MultinomialNB: Required non-negative input, so I used MinMaxScaler to scale all features between 0 and 1.
    • GaussianNB: Requires normally distributed continuous data, so I applied StandardScaler.
    • CategoricalNB: Required discrete inputs, so I used KBinsDiscretizer with n_bins=10 to convert numerical features into categorical bins.

    The dataset was split into training and testing sets using an 80:20 ratio via train_test_split with a fixed random_state for reproducibility. Ensuring that the training and testing data are disjoint is essential to avoid data leakage and to get an honest estimate of model performance on unseen data.

    Decision Tree Overview

    Decision Trees (DTs) are supervised machine learning models used for classification and regression tasks. They work by recursively splitting the data into subsets based on feature values, resulting in a tree-like model of decisions.

    Decision Trees are intuitive and interpretable, making them popular for applications like:

    • Credit scoring
    • Medical diagnosis
    • Fraud detection
    • Customer segmentation

    Sample decision tree structure showing feature-based splits

    Visual output of a trained decision tree classifier

    Splitting Criteria: GINI, Entropy, and Information Gain

    Decision Trees use criteria like Gini Impurity and Entropy to determine the best split. These metrics measure the impurity or uncertainty in the dataset.

    • Gini Impurity: Measures the likelihood of incorrect classification of a randomly chosen element. Formula: Gini = 1 - ∑(p_i^2)
    • Entropy: Measures the disorder or impurity. Formula: Entropy = -∑(p_i * log2(p_i))
    • Information Gain: The reduction in entropy or impurity from a split. It helps in selecting the attribute that best separates the samples.

    Example: Assume a binary classification dataset with 8 positive and 4 negative examples. Entropy before a split is:

    Entropy = - (8/12)*log2(8/12) - (4/12)*log2(4/12) ≈ 0.918

    If a split divides data into subsets with entropy values of 0.811 and 0.0, Information Gain is:

    IG = 0.918 - (9/12)*0.811 - (3/12)*0.0 = 0.309

    Since features can be split repeatedly in different ways, an infinite number of unique trees can be created depending on data combinations, depth, and criteria.

    Regression Overview

    (a) What is Linear Regression?

    Linear Regression is a supervised learning algorithm used to predict a continuous output variable based on one or more input features. It assumes a linear relationship between the independent variables (features) and the dependent variable (target). The model fits a straight line by minimizing the sum of squared differences between actual and predicted values. This is known as the least squares method.

    (b) What is Logistic Regression?

    Logistic Regression is a classification algorithm used to predict binary outcomes (e.g., 0 or 1, true or false). It computes a linear combination of input features and applies the sigmoid function to produce probabilities between 0 and 1. Based on a defined threshold (commonly 0.5), it classifies inputs into one of the two categories. Logistic regression is widely used in medical diagnoses, risk prediction, and binary classification tasks.

    (c) Similarities and Differences

    Both Linear and Logistic Regression use a linear combination of input features and are trained using labeled data. The key difference is that Linear Regression predicts continuous numerical values, while Logistic Regression predicts probabilities for classification. Linear Regression uses least squares for optimization, while Logistic Regression uses Maximum Likelihood Estimation. Additionally, Logistic Regression includes a non-linear transformation (sigmoid) to map values between 0 and 1.

    (d) Sigmoid Function in Logistic Regression

    Yes, Logistic Regression uses the sigmoid function to map the raw output of the linear model into a probability between 0 and 1. This is essential for classification because it interprets the output as the probability of belonging to the positive class. The sigmoid function is defined as 1 / (1 + e^-z), where z is the linear combination of inputs.

    (e) Maximum Likelihood in Logistic Regression

    Logistic Regression uses Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE) to estimate model parameters. MLE finds the set of weights that maximize the likelihood of observing the actual labels in the training data. Instead of minimizing error, this approach focuses on making the predicted probabilities closely match the true class labels. It is optimized using gradient descent or related techniques.
